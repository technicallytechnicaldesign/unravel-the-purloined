Research Packet + Product / Website Brainstorm
Version 0.2

======================================================================
PURPOSE OF THIS PACKET
======================================================================

This packet gathers the current research, historical leads, conceptual framework,
cipher/steganography ideas, generator/decoder proposals, and development roadmap
for a project about textiles as communication.

The project began with a simple question:

    Can a knitted object carry a secret message?

It quickly expanded into a much richer set of questions:

    - When has craft itself functioned as resistance?
    - When has a textile been a public political signal?
    - When has thread carried literal information?
    - What is the difference between encoding, ciphering, encryption,
      and steganography?
    - Which historical "secret textile" stories are well documented,
      and which are folklore?
    - What kinds of messages could intentionally be built into knitting today?
    - How can an app turn text into a genuinely knittable project?
    - How can the website teach both textile history and information theory?
    - How can users experiment with their own textile languages?

The current working vision is therefore not just "a Morse-code knitting app."

It is closer to:

    DIGITAL MUSEUM
    + CRAFT-RESISTANCE ARCHIVE
    + TEXTILE COMMUNICATION PRIMER
    + CIPHER / STEGANOGRAPHY LAB
    + KNITTING PATTERN GENERATOR
    + DECODER

Possible working titles include:

    STITCH / SIGNAL
    PURL//CODE
    THE HIDDEN STITCH
    KNIT CIPHER
    THREAD SIGNAL
    THREAD / CODE
    PURL RESISTANCE

STITCH / SIGNAL is used throughout this document as the placeholder title.

01. PROJECT THESIS
======================================================================

The central editorial question is:

    WHAT CAN A PIECE OF THREAD COMMUNICATE?

The answer is broader than "a hidden sentence."

A textile can communicate through:

    - its colour,
    - its shape,
    - its material,
    - the labour required to make it,
    - who made it,
    - who wears it,
    - when it is worn,
    - where it is displayed,
    - repeated visual motifs,
    - literal written or pictorial content,
    - stitch structure,
    - knots,
    - spacing,
    - orientation,
    - intentional mistakes,
    - and information encoded beneath the visible design.

The broader thesis of STITCH / SIGNAL could be:

Textiles have repeatedly functioned as communication technologies.
Sometimes they communicate openly. Sometimes indirectly. Sometimes secretly.
Sometimes the act of making is itself the political message.

A useful underlying idea is that textiles have historically been underestimated
as communication media partly because textile work itself has often been treated
as domestic, decorative, feminine, ordinary, or apolitical.

Yet thread can:

    - carry a slogan,
    - identify a movement,
    - enact a boycott,
    - preserve a prisoner's agency,
    - record testimony,
    - memorialize the dead,
    - coordinate strangers,
    - carry numbers,
    - encode words,
    - hide ciphertext,
    - and physically store data.

A knitting needle can therefore be framed, somewhat cheekily but usefully, as:

    A VERY SLOW WRITING DEVICE.


======================================================================
02. A TAXONOMY OF TEXTILE COMMUNICATION
======================================================================

To keep the site conceptually clear, it is useful to separate several different
ways craft can communicate.

----------------------------------------------------------------------
2.1 TEXTILE AS SYMBOL
----------------------------------------------------------------------

The textile itself has a collectively understood meaning.

Examples:
    - a red cap associated with Norwegian resistance,
    - a Pussyhat at the 2017 Women's March,
    - movement colours,
    - political banners,
    - garments tied to national or collective identity.

No hidden code is necessary.

The object effectively says:

    "I am part of this."
    "I oppose this."
    "I remember this."
    "I stand with these people."

----------------------------------------------------------------------
2.2 TEXTILE AS TESTIMONY OR DOCUMENT
----------------------------------------------------------------------

The textile records experiences, people, events, losses, or claims.

Examples:
    - Chilean arpilleras,
    - memorial quilts,
    - embroidered political scenes,
    - commemorative samplers.

The message is not necessarily secret.

The textile functions as an alternative archive.

----------------------------------------------------------------------
2.3 TEXTILE AS ECONOMIC ACTION
----------------------------------------------------------------------

The politics are embedded in production and consumption.

Examples:
    - homespun cloth used in anti-import campaigns,
    - spinning bees,
    - khadi and Swadeshi.

The message may be:

    "I refuse the supply chain you depend on."
    "I can make locally instead."
    "My clothing participates in a boycott."

----------------------------------------------------------------------
2.4 TEXTILE AS PUBLIC PERFORMANCE
----------------------------------------------------------------------

The visible act of making, gathering, knitting, spinning, sewing, or displaying
textiles becomes part of a protest.

Examples:
    - spinning bees,
    - knit-ins,
    - Knitting Nannas,
    - collective quilt displays.

Here, craft can communicate duration and persistence:

    WE ARE STILL HERE.
    WE HAVE TIME.
    WE ARE NOT LEAVING.

----------------------------------------------------------------------
2.5 TEXTILE AS COVERT CARRIER
----------------------------------------------------------------------

Literal information is embedded in the textile structure.

Possible carriers:
    - knit vs purl,
    - dropped stitch vs intact stitch,
    - knot vs no knot,
    - colour A vs colour B,
    - left cable vs right cable,
    - motif variant A vs motif variant B,
    - spacing between features.

This is where the project begins to overlap strongly with encoding and
steganography.

----------------------------------------------------------------------
2.6 TEXTILE AS CIPHERTEXT
----------------------------------------------------------------------

The textile may contain a message that has already been cryptographically
transformed.

For example:

    MEET AT NOON
        ->
    encrypted bytes
        ->
    binary
        ->
    knit/purl pattern

Someone who discovers the data still cannot necessarily understand the message.

----------------------------------------------------------------------
2.7 TEXTILE AS MEMORY / DATA STRUCTURE
----------------------------------------------------------------------

Thread can carry structured information without representing prose.

Examples:
    - counts,
    - quantities,
    - sequences,
    - ownership,
    - identity,
    - dates,
    - pattern instructions,
    - event records.

This category is especially useful when discussing khipu.

03. HISTORICAL EVIDENCE LABELS
======================================================================

One of the strongest editorial decisions for the website would be to make the
quality of historical evidence visible rather than smoothing everything into a
single confident narrative.

Recommended labels:

[A] PRIMARY / OBJECT EVIDENCE
    Surviving object, regulation, contemporary text, archival document,
    testimony, or other direct historical evidence.

[B] STRONG HISTORICAL RECONSTRUCTION
    Supported by archival scholarship or multiple reliable sources.

[C] LATER ACCOUNT / ORAL HISTORY
    Potentially valuable and meaningful, but harder to independently verify.

[D] DISPUTED / INSUFFICIENT EVIDENCE
    A claim exists but historians disagree, or the documentation is weak.

[X] EXPERIMENTAL RECONSTRUCTION
    A modern system built by STITCH / SIGNAL from a historical principle.

Possible user-facing badges:

    DOCUMENTED
    ARCHIVAL
    CONTEMPORARY SOURCE
    LATER ACCOUNT
    ORAL HISTORY
    DISPUTED
    FOLKLORE / UNVERIFIED
    MODERN RECONSTRUCTION
    EXPERIMENTAL

This evidence layer is especially important because secret communication stories
are naturally prone to romantic retelling. A good story can migrate from
"possible" to "everyone knows this happened" surprisingly quickly.

04. HISTORICAL CORE: NORWAY AND RESISTANCE KNITTING
======================================================================

The Norwegian story is a very strong starting point, but it should be presented
accurately.

The clearest documented connection is not currently "Norwegian resistance women
knitted Morse code into garments."

Instead, Norway gives us a powerful history of textiles as:

    - national symbolism,
    - community identity,
    - self-production,
    - prohibited political expression,
    - and resistance.

The red knitted topplue / nisselue became associated with Norwegian identity and
resistance during the German occupation.

Store norske leksikon describes the red topplue as a symbol of Norwegian
identity and resistance. It also records a police prohibition that took effect
on 26 February 1942 after the use of red caps had become so widespread that
authorities treated wearing them as a demonstration. [S01]

The importance of this example lies in how little technology it required.

There is no cipher.

There is no secret key.

The transformation is social:

    ordinary cap
        ->
    shared symbol
        ->
    political signal
        ->
    prohibited symbol

That is an excellent introductory case for the website because it immediately
shows that "communication" does not have to mean literal written language.

A possible interactive page could ask:

    WHEN DOES AN OBJECT BECOME A MESSAGE?

and visually walk through:

    warm hat
    -> national association
    -> widespread coordinated use
    -> political recognition
    -> prohibition

05. GRINI: CLANDESTINE MAKING AS RESISTANCE
======================================================================

MiA's material on Grini provides one of the strongest sources for the broader
idea of craft as resistance. [S02]

Women prisoners made tiny knitted and embroidered objects using extremely
limited means.

Documented techniques and materials include:

    - very small mittens and socks,
    - improvised knitting tools such as hairpins or sewing pins,
    - thread salvaged from clothing, bedding, and other available material,
    - patriotic motifs and statements,
    - hidden manufacture because some objects were dangerous to possess.

MiA's interpretation is particularly useful because it goes beyond overt
political iconography.

The museum frames hidden handwork as a way prisoners could preserve:

    - concentration,
    - imagination,
    - a feeling of competence,
    - control,
    - self-respect,
    - and inner freedom.

This suggests an important website theme:

    RESISTANCE DOES NOT REQUIRE AN AUDIENCE.

An object can function as resistance even if nobody outside the prison sees it.

Making can preserve identity against a system designed to strip identity away.

This gives STITCH / SIGNAL a much deeper scope than secret codes alone.


======================================================================
06. AGNES "AGGI" KLØNIG:
    MESSAGE-SMUGGLING AND CLANDESTINE NEEDLEWORK
======================================================================

Agnes Klønig is an especially useful case because she connects two themes:

    clandestine communication
    +
    clandestine textile work

Available museum material and earlier research leads describe Klønig as involved
in carrying messages through her Red Cross access before imprisonment, and as
later making clandestine needlework while imprisoned.

IMPORTANT HISTORICAL CAUTION:

These are two documented or reported activities that must not automatically be
collapsed into the stronger claim:

    "Klønig encoded her smuggled messages in knitting."

At present, that stronger claim has not been established in this project.

For publication, the site should say something like:

    "Klønig's story brings message-smuggling and clandestine needlework into the
    same biography, but we have not found evidence that her messages themselves
    were encoded in stitches."

This is exactly the kind of distinction the evidence-badge system is designed
to preserve.


======================================================================
07. BELGIUM, WWI, AND THE "KNITTING SPY" STORY
======================================================================

The famous story of women watching trains while knitting is much more strongly
associated with occupied Belgium during the First World War than with Norway in
the Second.

Belgian intelligence networks, including La Dame Blanche / The White Lady,
monitored German transport and passed information to British intelligence.

Historian Helen Fry's recent history of the White Lady network is based on newly
available archival material and provides a strong modern research route into
the network as a whole. [S03]

Popular and educational retellings also describe knitting being used to record
train movements, with different stitch types marking different observations.

However, the exact, widely repeated anecdote of an anonymous woman sitting by a
window and dropping stitches for certain trains should be treated carefully.

Recommended site treatment:

[B] WELL-SUPPORTED:
    Belgian intelligence networks monitored railway activity and transmitted
    information.

[C/D] NEEDS PRECISE SOURCE VERIFICATION BEFORE STRONG PUBLICATION CLAIM:
    specific stitch-by-stitch systems attributed to particular anonymous
    knitters.

This could become a wonderful "how history mutates" page:

    ARCHIVE
    ->
    RETELLING
    ->
    POPULAR ANECDOTE
    ->
    INTERNET FACTOID

The page could invite users to inspect which parts are documented and which are
still being researched.


======================================================================
08. WARTIME HANDWORK CODES AS A HISTORICAL RESEARCH LEAD
======================================================================

The Smithsonian catalog confirms the existence of:

    Gordon A. J. Petersen and Marshall McClintock
    A Guide to Codes and Signals
    1942

The book covers signals, Morse, signs, symbols, and ciphers. [S04]

Earlier secondary research around the project points toward a section on codes
in handwork, including techniques involving knitting, embroidery, rugs, and
knot placement.

Before quoting or presenting any specific knot system as established fact on
the final website, the relevant original pages should be obtained and checked
directly.

That makes this a HIGH-PRIORITY PRIMARY-SOURCE TASK.

Why this source is exciting:

It suggests a form of textile steganography where the completed object is not
necessarily what is decoded.

One reported concept is:

    mark yarn with knots at meaningful intervals
    ->
    knit it into an ordinary object
    ->
    recipient unravels the object
    ->
    read the knot spacing against a key

That inspires an excellent experimental tool even if we ultimately label our
specific implementation [X] MODERN RECONSTRUCTION.

Possible feature name:

    DESTRUCTIVE DECODE MODE

Possible warning:

    DECRYPTION REQUIRES FROGGING.


======================================================================
09. HOMESPUN, SPINNING, AND ECONOMIC RESISTANCE
======================================================================

In colonial North America, political resistance to British taxation and trade
policy included non-importation and non-consumption campaigns.

Textiles mattered because imported cloth could be replaced through household
production.

Smithsonian and National Park Service material documents homespun production,
spinning bees, and the political significance of women increasing domestic
textile production during boycotts. [S05] [S06]

This provides a distinct mechanism of textile resistance:

    not "the cloth says something"
    but
    "making the cloth changes the economic relationship"

The act of spinning becomes part of a boycott.

Possible website concept:

    RESISTANCE IN THE SUPPLY CHAIN

Questions:
    - Who made the material?
    - Who normally profits from the purchase?
    - What happens when consumers deliberately substitute local production?
    - Can labour itself become a political signal?


======================================================================
10. KHADI AND ANTI-COLONIAL TEXTILE PRODUCTION
======================================================================

The Indian Swadeshi movement promoted rejection of foreign goods and support for
domestic production.

The V&A describes Gandhi's call to spin, weave, and wear khadi, and notes that
khadi became a symbol of resistance. In 1921, Indian nationalists incorporated
the spinning wheel into their flag. [S07]

Again, the textile does several things simultaneously:

    economic substitution,
    visible political symbolism,
    material self-reliance,
    mass participation.

This makes khadi an important counterpoint to secret-message stories.

It shows that a textile can function politically without hiding anything.


======================================================================
11. SUFFRAGE BANNERS AND PUBLIC TEXTILE MESSAGING
======================================================================

Textile banners were highly visible elements of the British women's suffrage
movement.

London Museum preserves examples from the 1908 NUWSS procession, including the
Women Writers' Suffrage League banner designed by Mary Lowndes and embroidered
by Christiana Herringham. [S08]

The medium matters.

Textile techniques associated with domestic or decorative labour were brought
into public political demonstration.

Mechanism:

    domestic skill
    ->
    monumental public object
    ->
    procession
    ->
    mass political visibility

This deserves a section on:

    WHEN PRIVATE TECHNIQUES ENTER PUBLIC SPACE


======================================================================
12. CHILEAN ARPILLERAS: TEXTILE AS TESTIMONY
======================================================================

During Chile's dictatorship, arpilleras became a medium through which women
depicted experiences including repression, disappearance, poverty, detention,
and daily life.

The Museo de la Memoria y los Derechos Humanos treats arpilleras as part of
resistance, organization, and testimony. [S09]

The textile becomes:

    image,
    witness,
    portable archive,
    social record.

This suggests another website category:

    WHEN THE TEXTILE IS THE DOCUMENT.

The message is not necessarily encrypted.

Its power lies in what it records and how it travels.


======================================================================
13. THE AIDS MEMORIAL QUILT:
    ACCUMULATION AS COMMUNICATION
======================================================================

The AIDS Memorial Quilt demonstrates a completely different communication
mechanism.

The first major National Mall display in 1987 contained 1,920 panels. [S10]

Each panel memorialized a person.

The combined work transformed individual handmade objects into a physical
representation of collective loss.

Communication mechanism:

    one maker
    -> one person
    -> one panel

then:

    thousands of panels
    -> scale itself becomes the message

This is a useful category for STITCH / SIGNAL:

    ACCUMULATION AS COMMUNICATION.

A single handmade object can be intimate.

Thousands together become impossible to ignore.


======================================================================
14. PUSSYHATS:
    DISTRIBUTED PATTERN AS POLITICAL INFRASTRUCTURE
======================================================================

The Pussyhat Project is especially relevant because the pattern itself was a
distribution technology.

The V&A records the knitted pink hat as a major symbol of the 21 January 2017
Women's March and describes the deliberate goal of creating a visible "sea of
pink." It also documents the public sharing of the simple pattern so people
could make or donate hats. [S11]

Mechanism:

    pattern
    ->
    distributed makers
    ->
    repeated object
    ->
    mass visual field

This parallels the architecture of STITCH / SIGNAL.

Our own generated pattern is also an instruction set capable of turning
information into a distributed physical object.


======================================================================
15. KNITTING NANNAS:
    CRAFT AS PRESENCE AND DURATION
======================================================================

The Australian Knitting Nannas began in 2012 in opposition to coal-seam gas
development.

Their own history describes knitting accompanying observation of company
activity and evolving into an element of public environmental activism. [S12]

This creates another communication mechanism:

    craft as a way to remain present.

Knitting is slow.

That can be a disadvantage if the goal is fast production.

It can be an advantage if the goal is:

    occupation,
    observation,
    persistence,
    visibility,
    refusing to leave.

Possible website theme:

    CRAFT AS DURATION.


======================================================================
16. KHIPU / QUIPU:
    THREAD AS STRUCTURED DATA
======================================================================

Khipu are a crucial example for the project's broader "thread as information"
theme.

Inka khipu use cords, knots, arrangement, and other material properties to
record structured information. Surviving museum examples are preserved by
institutions including the Smithsonian's National Museum of the American Indian.
[S13]

The project should NOT imply that khipu are direct ancestors of coded European
knitting.

Instead, the lesson is broader:

Humans have repeatedly discovered that thread offers multiple variables that can
store information.

Possible variables include:

    knot type,
    knot position,
    cord colour,
    cord hierarchy,
    spacing,
    direction,
    grouping.

This is conceptually powerful because it moves the project beyond the idea that
"data must look like writing."


======================================================================
17. UNDERGROUND RAILROAD QUILT CODES:
    EVIDENCE VS FOLKLORE
======================================================================

The commonly repeated story that standardized quilt patterns were used to
communicate routes and instructions on the Underground Railroad is disputed.

The Smithsonian Center for Folklife gives a nuanced overview of the story's
modern popularity and the lack of strong contemporary documentation for a
standardized code system. [S14]

This should become a flagship evidence-literacy page.

Suggested title:

    GOOD STORY. WHERE'S THE EVIDENCE?

The point should not be to ridicule oral tradition.

The point is to distinguish:

    "This story exists and matters culturally"

from:

    "We can prove this system was widely used historically."

This is exactly why STITCH / SIGNAL should expose evidence grades.


======================================================================
18. GENERAL INFORMATION THEORY FOR THE PROJECT
======================================================================

The experimental half of the site becomes much clearer if users first understand
that several different operations can happen between a human message and a
finished textile.

A useful general pipeline is:

    HUMAN MESSAGE
        ->
    NORMALIZATION
        ->
    OPTIONAL CRYPTOGRAPHIC TRANSFORMATION
        ->
    DATA ENCODING
        ->
    ERROR-PROTECTION LAYER
        ->
    TEXTILE SYMBOL MAPPING
        ->
    LAYOUT / CAMOUFLAGE
        ->
    KNITTING TRANSLATION
        ->
    PHYSICAL OBJECT

Example:

    "MEET AT NOON"
        ->
    "MEET AT NOON"
        ->
    Vigenere ciphertext
        ->
    letter values
        ->
    binary
        ->
    parity/check symbols
        ->
    knit/purl cells
        ->
    chart
        ->
    flat-knitting row instructions
        ->
    scarf

Every arrow is a different process.

That matters because users often use words like "code," "cipher," and
"encryption" interchangeably even though they describe different things.


======================================================================
19. ENCODING VS DECODING
======================================================================

ENCODING is a representation problem.

It answers:

    "How do I represent this information using another symbol system?"

Examples:
    - text -> Morse
    - letter -> 5-bit value
    - byte -> binary
    - binary 0 -> knit
    - binary 1 -> purl

Encoding does not automatically provide secrecy.

If everyone knows the mapping, anyone can decode it.

DECODING reverses the representation.

Example:

    K P K P P
        ->
    0 1 0 1 1
        ->
    value 11
        ->
    letter L

The site should repeatedly reinforce:

    ENCODED != ENCRYPTED.


======================================================================
20. CODES, CIPHERS, ENCRYPTION, CRYPTOGRAPHY, AND CRYPTANALYSIS
======================================================================

----------------------------------------------------------------------
20.1 CODE
----------------------------------------------------------------------

Historically, "code" can mean many things.

One useful distinction is:

A CODE may map whole units of meaning to other units.

Example:

    APPLE = "attack"
    BRIDGE = "retreat"
    4821 = "train leaving at dawn"

Codebooks historically allowed phrases or concepts to be replaced by shorter or
less obvious tokens.

In casual usage, "code" is also used much more broadly for any systematic
representation.

For STITCH / SIGNAL, it is fine to use "code" conversationally, but educational
pages should distinguish it from a cipher.

----------------------------------------------------------------------
20.2 CIPHER
----------------------------------------------------------------------

A cipher systematically transforms symbols according to a rule.

Examples:
    - Caesar shift,
    - monoalphabetic substitution,
    - Vigenere,
    - transposition systems,
    - modern encryption algorithms.

The result is CIPHERTEXT.

The original message is PLAINTEXT.

----------------------------------------------------------------------
20.3 ENCRYPTION
----------------------------------------------------------------------

NIST defines encryption as a cryptographic transformation that turns plaintext
into ciphertext, typically using a cryptographic algorithm and key. [S15]

Encryption aims to protect the meaning of the information.

Modern encryption should rely on established, reviewed algorithms rather than
home-made substitution schemes.

----------------------------------------------------------------------
20.4 CRYPTOGRAPHY
----------------------------------------------------------------------

NIST describes cryptography as the discipline concerned with transforming and
protecting information, including goals such as confidentiality, integrity, and
authentication. [S16]

For this project, a simplified explanation is:

    Cryptography changes information in order to protect or verify it.

----------------------------------------------------------------------
20.5 CRYPTANALYSIS
----------------------------------------------------------------------

Cryptanalysis is the study of recovering information from cryptographic systems,
especially without already possessing the intended key.

On the educational site, classical cipher-breaking activities could include:

    - frequency analysis,
    - looking for repeated patterns,
    - guessing common words,
    - comparing probable plaintext,
    - exploiting short or repeated keys.

----------------------------------------------------------------------
20.6 CRYPTOLOGY
----------------------------------------------------------------------

Cryptology is the broader field that includes both:

    cryptography
    +
    cryptanalysis.


======================================================================
21. STEGANOGRAPHY
======================================================================

NIST describes steganography as communicating in a way that hides the existence
of the communication. [S17]

This distinction is central to the project.

CRYPTOGRAPHY says:

    "You can see that a message exists, but you should not be able to understand
    it."

STEGANOGRAPHY says:

    "Ideally, you do not realize there is a message at all."

These can be combined.

Example:

    plaintext
        ->
    encrypt
        ->
    ciphertext
        ->
    hide ciphertext inside a decorative knitting motif

Now the observer must first notice that data is present before even reaching the
encrypted content.

This layered model should be one of the main educational diagrams on the site.


======================================================================
22. CLASSICAL CIPHER FAMILIES
======================================================================

This section provides candidate methods for educational generators.

IMPORTANT:
These are excellent for history, puzzle-solving, and experimentation.
They should not be presented as secure modern encryption.

----------------------------------------------------------------------
22.1 CAESAR / SHIFT CIPHER
----------------------------------------------------------------------

Each letter is shifted by a fixed amount.

Example with shift +3:

    A -> D
    B -> E
    C -> F

Advantages:
    - extremely easy to teach,
    - very easy to implement,
    - useful for demonstrating the concept of a key.

Weakness:
    - trivial to break.

Textile use:
    plaintext -> Caesar -> 5-bit -> knit/purl

----------------------------------------------------------------------
22.2 MONOALPHABETIC SUBSTITUTION
----------------------------------------------------------------------

Each plaintext letter maps to a different ciphertext symbol or letter.

Example:

    A -> Q
    B -> M
    C -> T

The substitution alphabet becomes the key.

Advantages:
    - intuitive,
    - fun for puzzles,
    - can generate a custom "secret alphabet."

Weakness:
    - vulnerable to frequency analysis on longer messages.

Textile use:
    ideal for a "make your own knitted alphabet" tool.

----------------------------------------------------------------------
22.3 KEYWORD SUBSTITUTION
----------------------------------------------------------------------

A keyword is used to construct a reordered alphabet.

Useful because the user can remember the key more easily than a fully random
substitution table.

Again:
    educational, not secure.

----------------------------------------------------------------------
22.4 HOMOPHONIC SUBSTITUTION
----------------------------------------------------------------------

Common letters can map to several possible symbols.

Example:

    E -> 04 / 17 / 62
    T -> 11 / 29
    Q -> 81

This flattens obvious letter-frequency patterns.

Textile potential:
    the same letter could be represented by several visually related motif
    variants, making repetition less obvious.

----------------------------------------------------------------------
22.5 POLYALPHABETIC CIPHERS
----------------------------------------------------------------------

Instead of one substitution alphabet, several alphabets are used.

The Vigenere cipher is the best-known educational example.

A repeating key changes how each successive letter is transformed.

Textile use:
    a historically flavored "keyword cipher" mode.

Educational value:
    demonstrates why repeated keys are better than one fixed shift but still
    create patterns an analyst can exploit.

----------------------------------------------------------------------
22.6 TRANSPOSITION CIPHERS
----------------------------------------------------------------------

The symbols are not replaced.

Their positions are rearranged.

Simple examples:
    - rail fence,
    - columnar transposition,
    - route ciphers.

This maps wonderfully to textiles because textiles already have a physical grid.

Possible textile interpretation:

    write message bits into a grid row-wise
    but read them according to a secret route:
        spiral,
        columns,
        diagonal,
        serpentine,
        checkerboard,
        outside-in.

This could produce a very satisfying "route cipher scarf."

----------------------------------------------------------------------
22.7 BACON'S BILITERAL CIPHER
----------------------------------------------------------------------

Francis Bacon described a system based on groups of five binary choices:
traditionally A/B forms.

NSA historical material describes the cipher as representing alphabetic letters
using five-position A/B combinations. [S18]

This is extremely relevant to knitting.

Map:

    A-form = knit
    B-form = purl

or:

    A-form = colour 1
    B-form = colour 2

The resulting textile can use a historically grounded five-state structure while
still being a modern textile reconstruction.

This is one of the best candidate "history -> interactive generator" links.

----------------------------------------------------------------------
22.8 POLYBIUS-SQUARE-STYLE FRACTIONATION
----------------------------------------------------------------------

Letters are placed into a grid and represented by coordinates.

Example:

    A = 11
    B = 12
    ...

Textile potential:

    row coordinate = colour
    column coordinate = stitch texture

or:

    first digit = motif family
    second digit = orientation

This lets one character be split across multiple visual properties.

----------------------------------------------------------------------
22.9 NULL CIPHERS
----------------------------------------------------------------------

The visible message contains an apparently ordinary text, but hidden information
is extracted by a rule.

Example:
    read every third word,
    read first letter of each sentence,
    read every nth character.

Textile analogue:

    only every fifth motif matters,
    only stitches at the edge matter,
    only the first purl after each cable matters.

This is very close to textile steganography.

----------------------------------------------------------------------
22.10 GRILLE / MASK CIPHERS
----------------------------------------------------------------------

A physical or conceptual mask determines which positions in a larger field are
meaningful.

Textile version:

    a decorative colourwork field
    +
    a printable decoder overlay
    ->
    only selected cells reveal the message

Possible tool:

    GENERATE DECODER GRILLE

This could be visually spectacular.

----------------------------------------------------------------------
22.11 BOOK / REFERENCE CIPHERS
----------------------------------------------------------------------

Numbers point to positions in a shared text.

Example:
    page / line / word.

Textile version:

    knitted numbers refer to a shared book or pattern.

This would dramatically reduce the amount of textile data required because the
garment stores references rather than the entire sentence.

It is also historically interesting because codebooks and reference systems were
widely used in communications.

----------------------------------------------------------------------
22.12 ONE-TIME PAD CONCEPT
----------------------------------------------------------------------

A true one-time pad combines plaintext with a genuinely random key that:

    - is at least as long as the message,
    - is never reused,
    - remains secret.

Correctly used, it offers exceptional theoretical secrecy.

For the site:
    teach the concept,
    perhaps demonstrate it,
    but do not casually call improvised pseudo-random textile keys a "one-time
    pad" unless they actually meet the requirements.

Possible delightful textile experiment:

    knit one half of the key into one object
    and the ciphertext into another.

Educational, not operational advice.


======================================================================
23. MODERN CRYPTOGRAPHY:
    WHAT THE SITE SHOULD AND SHOULD NOT CLAIM
======================================================================

If STITCH / SIGNAL ever includes a mode labelled "secure encryption," the
cryptography should not be invented by us.

The system should use a standard browser cryptography implementation.

Conceptually:

    message
        ->
    established authenticated encryption
        ->
    ciphertext bytes
        ->
    binary/textile representation

A strong web implementation could eventually use established Web Crypto
primitives.

A likely design direction would involve:

    - authenticated encryption,
    - a properly derived key,
    - random salt / nonce values as required by the algorithm,
    - all cryptographic processing in the browser where practical,
    - no claim that the textile carrier itself adds meaningful cryptographic
      strength.

A crucial wording distinction:

    HANDMADE CIPHER MODE
        = puzzle / education / historical experimentation

    MODERN ENCRYPTION MODE
        = uses established cryptographic primitives

Avoid phrases such as:

    "unbreakable knitting cipher"

unless the statement is very carefully scoped.

The textile is a STORAGE / TRANSMISSION MEDIUM.

The security should come from the cryptography, not from the obscurity of
knitting.


======================================================================
24. ERROR DETECTION, CORRECTION, AND NOISY TEXTILE CHANNELS
======================================================================

Textiles are physical media.

Physical media are noisy.

A knitter can:

    - miss a stitch,
    - add a stitch,
    - swap colours,
    - twist a cable,
    - misread a chart,
    - accidentally repeat a row,
    - drop a stitch,
    - rotate the finished object,
    - start decoding from the wrong edge.

That makes communications engineering unexpectedly relevant.

----------------------------------------------------------------------
24.1 FRAMING
----------------------------------------------------------------------

Use distinctive START and END markers.

Purpose:
    tell the decoder where the message begins and ends.

----------------------------------------------------------------------
24.2 ORIENTATION MARKERS
----------------------------------------------------------------------

Add an asymmetric marker that identifies:

    top,
    bottom,
    left,
    right,
    right side,
    wrong side.

This prevents accidental decoding upside down or backwards.

----------------------------------------------------------------------
24.3 CHARACTER SEPARATORS
----------------------------------------------------------------------

Fixed spacing between symbols reduces synchronization errors.

Example:

    five data cells
    + one separator cell

----------------------------------------------------------------------
24.4 PARITY
----------------------------------------------------------------------

Add one extra bit that records whether the number of 1s is odd or even.

Parity cannot repair an error, but it can detect many single-bit errors.

Textile example:

    five letter bits
    + one check stitch.

----------------------------------------------------------------------
24.5 CHECKSUM
----------------------------------------------------------------------

Generate a final value from the message.

The decoder recomputes the checksum and compares it.

If it differs:

    SOMETHING WENT WRONG.

----------------------------------------------------------------------
24.6 REDUNDANCY
----------------------------------------------------------------------

Repeat critical information.

Examples:
    - each character twice,
    - repeat the key at both ends,
    - majority vote across three copies.

This costs space but makes hand-made communication much more robust.

----------------------------------------------------------------------
24.7 ERROR-CORRECTING CODES
----------------------------------------------------------------------

More advanced versions could use actual error-correcting codes.

A future research branch could experiment with Hamming-style codes.

This would allow the system not merely to say:

    "there is probably an error"

but potentially:

    "this specific bit is wrong."

That is hilariously appropriate for knitting:

    ERROR NEAR STITCH 83.

----------------------------------------------------------------------
24.8 SYNCHRONIZATION
----------------------------------------------------------------------

Missing or added stitches can shift every subsequent symbol.

Fixed-width blocks and frequent framing markers are therefore extremely useful.

Possible robust format:

    START
    [5 data + parity + separator]
    [5 data + parity + separator]
    ...
    ROW MARKER
    ...
    END
    CHECKSUM


======================================================================
25. WAYS TEXTILES CAN PHYSICALLY STORE INFORMATION
======================================================================

This is the heart of the experimental design space.

A textile unit can carry information through many independent variables.

----------------------------------------------------------------------
25.1 PRESENCE / ABSENCE
----------------------------------------------------------------------

    bobble / no bobble
    bead / no bead
    eyelet / no eyelet
    knot / no knot

Binary by nature.

----------------------------------------------------------------------
25.2 TEXTURE
----------------------------------------------------------------------

    knit / purl
    seed / stockinette
    raised / recessed
    smooth / fuzzy material

----------------------------------------------------------------------
25.3 COLOUR
----------------------------------------------------------------------

    colour A / colour B
    multiple colours
    brightness categories
    stripe lengths

Two colours = 1 bit per controlled unit.

Four reliably distinguishable states can represent 2 bits.

Eight states can represent 3 bits.

----------------------------------------------------------------------
25.4 ORIENTATION
----------------------------------------------------------------------

    left cable / right cable
    slash / backslash
    leaf left / leaf right
    diagonal NW-SE / NE-SW

----------------------------------------------------------------------
25.5 SIZE
----------------------------------------------------------------------

    small bobble / large bobble
    short stripe / long stripe
    2-stitch motif / 4-stitch motif

Potentially useful, but harder to read reliably.

----------------------------------------------------------------------
25.6 SPACING
----------------------------------------------------------------------

The distance between features carries information.

Examples:
    knot spacing,
    rows between stripes,
    stitches between beads.

Excellent for destructive yarn codes.

----------------------------------------------------------------------
25.7 COUNT
----------------------------------------------------------------------

A number of repeated elements represents a value.

Example:
    1 bobble = A
    2 bobbles = B
    3 bobbles = C

Simple but low-density.

----------------------------------------------------------------------
25.8 ORDER
----------------------------------------------------------------------

A sequence of otherwise ordinary motifs can be significant.

Example:
    flower / leaf / leaf / star.

----------------------------------------------------------------------
25.9 MATERIAL
----------------------------------------------------------------------

Different fibres or yarns could encode categories.

Example:
    wool / cotton
    matte / reflective
    conductive / ordinary yarn

Potentially difficult for visual decoding but interesting conceptually.

----------------------------------------------------------------------
25.10 TWIST / DIRECTION
----------------------------------------------------------------------

Yarn twist, cable direction, or ply direction can theoretically carry state.

Likely too subtle for a basic knitting app, but worth documenting.

----------------------------------------------------------------------
25.11 STRUCTURAL CHOICE
----------------------------------------------------------------------

Two techniques that appear visually similar could encode different values.

Example:
    left-leaning decrease / right-leaning decrease.

This is particularly promising for lace steganography.

----------------------------------------------------------------------
25.12 LOCATION
----------------------------------------------------------------------

The information is determined by WHERE something appears rather than WHAT it is.

Examples:
    motif appears in one of two possible columns,
    bead is above/below centre,
    hidden symbol appears only along one edge.

----------------------------------------------------------------------
25.13 MULTIPLE CHANNELS
----------------------------------------------------------------------

Several properties can operate simultaneously.

Example:

    colour       = bit 1
    knit/purl    = bit 2
    cable lean   = bit 3

Eight possible combinations:

    2 x 2 x 2 = 8 states = 3 bits per unit

This leads to the deliberately ridiculous concept of:

    MULTI-CHANNEL TEXTILE DATA.


======================================================================
26. INFORMATION DENSITY AND TEXTILE CAPACITY
======================================================================

A useful educational lab could calculate how much theoretical information a
textile carrier can store.

Basic rule:

    bits per unit = log2(number of distinguishable states)

Examples:

2 states:
    knit / purl
    = 1 bit per cell

4 states:
    black / white x knit / purl
    = 2 bits per cell

8 states:
    2 colours x 2 textures x 2 directions
    = 3 bits per cell

The site should then introduce the important difference between:

    THEORETICAL CAPACITY
    and
    PRACTICAL CAPACITY.

A system with 16 tiny distinctions may mathematically store more information,
but if humans cannot reliably knit or distinguish them, it is useless.

A proposed comparison panel:

    METHOD              DENSITY   KNIT DIFFICULTY   READABILITY   CAMOUFLAGE
    knit/purl            low      easy              high          low
    2-colour             low      easy-medium       high          low
    cable direction      low      medium            medium        medium
    motif mutation       medium   medium-high       medium        high
    multi-channel        high     cursed            low           high

Yes, "cursed" can remain an internal working classification.


======================================================================
27. PROPOSED ENCODER / GENERATOR FAMILIES
======================================================================

----------------------------------------------------------------------
27.1 MORSE RELIEF GENERATOR
----------------------------------------------------------------------

Purpose:
    easiest entry point.

Input:
    text

Logical output:
    Morse dots and dashes.

Possible textile mappings:

    dot  = one purl bump
    dash = three purl bumps
    gap  = knit space

Alternative carriers:

    colour,
    bobble,
    bead,
    lace hole,
    stripe length.

Outputs:
    - Morse transcription,
    - stitch chart,
    - written rows,
    - visual preview,
    - optional decoder key.

Potential issue:
    Morse is variable-length.

Solution:
    clear symbol and letter spacing.

----------------------------------------------------------------------
27.2 FIVE-BIT KNIT/PURL GENERATOR
----------------------------------------------------------------------

Purpose:
    core general-purpose textile alphabet.

Five binary positions give:

    2^5 = 32 states.

Enough for:
    26 letters
    + space
    + several control symbols.

Example custom mapping:

    00000 = A
    00001 = B
    00010 = C
    ...

Carrier:

    0 = knit
    1 = purl

Major advantage:
    EVERY CHARACTER HAS THE SAME WIDTH.

This is excellent for charts.

Possible control values:

    SPACE
    START
    END
    NEW LINE
    punctuation
    escape / shift

----------------------------------------------------------------------
27.3 BACON-STYLE BILITERAL MODE
----------------------------------------------------------------------

A historically framed five-position A/B mode.

Carrier examples:

    A = knit
    B = purl

or:

    A = dark
    B = light

This is an ideal "learn history -> make a textile" generator.

----------------------------------------------------------------------
27.4 COLOURWORK BINARY GENERATOR
----------------------------------------------------------------------

Same underlying bits as five-bit mode.

Carrier:

    0 = background colour
    1 = contrast colour

Project types:

    scarf
    cowl
    hat
    mitten band
    sock band
    square swatch

Potential implementation techniques:

    stranded colourwork
    double knitting
    mosaic/slip stitch
    duplicate stitch

Duplicate stitch is especially attractive because the base object can be knitted
first and the data added afterward.

----------------------------------------------------------------------
27.5 BRAILLE / TACTILE MODE
----------------------------------------------------------------------

Two distinct modes should exist.

MODE A: ACTUAL BRAILLE
    educational / accessibility-oriented.

Possible textile dots:
    bobble
    bead
    raised knot

MODE B: SIX-DOT CUSTOM CIPHER
    uses the same 2x3 geometry but a custom alphabet.

Important:
    do not label arbitrary raised dots as readable Braille.
    Real tactile accessibility has spacing, height, and usability requirements
    that require dedicated research and testing.

----------------------------------------------------------------------
27.6 CABLE CIPHER
----------------------------------------------------------------------

Simple binary:

    left cable  = 0
    right cable = 1

This may look more naturally decorative than blocky knit/purl data.

More advanced possibilities:

    cable width,
    crossing frequency,
    number of strands.

Potential issue:
    increases knitting complexity rapidly.

----------------------------------------------------------------------
27.7 LACE CIPHER
----------------------------------------------------------------------

Candidate binary states:

    left-leaning decrease  = 0
    right-leaning decrease = 1

or:

    eyelet position left / right.

Challenge:
    maintain stitch count and fabric stability.

Strength:
    very good potential for visual camouflage.

----------------------------------------------------------------------
27.8 BOBBLE / BEAD CIPHER
----------------------------------------------------------------------

Presence / absence coding.

Advantages:
    - visually obvious,
    - tactile,
    - easy to decode manually.

Disadvantages:
    - relatively low density,
    - may alter drape.

----------------------------------------------------------------------
27.9 STRIPE-INTERVAL CODE
----------------------------------------------------------------------

Information stored in row counts.

Example:

    1 row  = value 1
    2 rows = value 2
    etc.

Or Morse:

    short stripe = dot
    long stripe  = dash

Excellent for:
    scarves,
    socks,
    hats,
    sleeves.

----------------------------------------------------------------------
27.10 KNOT-SPACING / DESTRUCTIVE CODE
----------------------------------------------------------------------

Message determines distances between knots in the working yarn.

The yarn is then knitted into an apparently ordinary item.

Decoder:

    unravel object
    ->
    measure intervals
    ->
    convert distances to symbols

Possible UI label:

    DESTRUCTIVE DECODE

Possible warning:

    THIS MESSAGE WILL REQUIRE FROGGING.

----------------------------------------------------------------------
27.11 MOTIF ALPHABET
----------------------------------------------------------------------

Each character is represented by a small graphic motif.

Possible grids:

    3x3
    4x4
    5x5

Style packs:

    geometric
    botanical
    pixel
    angular
    Nordic-inspired geometric
    glitch
    goblin mode

Important:
    do not call invented motifs "traditional" unless they are actually sourced
    from a documented tradition.

----------------------------------------------------------------------
27.12 STEGANOGRAPHIC MOTIF MUTATION
----------------------------------------------------------------------

This is one of the most promising long-term systems.

Start with a decorative repeating pattern.

Each motif has two visually plausible versions.

Example:

    0 = leaf leans left
    1 = leaf leans right

or:

    0 = centre empty
    1 = centre filled

or:

    0 = branch has 3 cells
    1 = branch has 4 cells

The user sees:

    decorative irregularity.

The decoder sees:

    bits.

This is much closer to true steganography than obvious binary colour blocks.

----------------------------------------------------------------------
27.13 ROUTE-CIPHER CHART
----------------------------------------------------------------------

Write data into a rectangular field.

Use a secret route to determine reading order.

Routes:

    row
    column
    snake
    spiral
    diagonal
    clockwise border
    centre-out
    checkerboard

The textile can look like a generic grid while the reading path acts as a key.

----------------------------------------------------------------------
27.14 GRILLE CIPHER TEXTILE
----------------------------------------------------------------------

Generate a decorative field and a separate decoder mask.

Only cells visible through the grille matter.

Outputs:

    garment chart
    +
    printable decoder grille

A very tactile, physical cipher experience.

----------------------------------------------------------------------
27.15 MULTI-CHANNEL MODE
----------------------------------------------------------------------

Use multiple independent textile properties.

Example:

    texture = data stream A
    colour  = data stream B

Potentially:

    data stream A = encrypted message
    data stream B = checksum
    border motifs  = key identifier

This should be a late-stage experimental mode because usability could collapse
very quickly.

28. PROPOSED DECODER SYSTEM
======================================================================

The decoder should not simply output plaintext.

It should expose intermediate steps.

Example:

    TEXTILE GRID
        ->
    K P K P P
        ->
    0 1 0 1 1
        ->
    01011
        ->
    value 11
        ->
    L

That makes the decoder educational.

Recommended modes:

----------------------------------------------------------------------
28.1 MANUAL GRID ENTRY
----------------------------------------------------------------------

Click cells to mark:

    knit / purl
    colour A / B
    left / right
    filled / empty

----------------------------------------------------------------------
28.2 PASTE PROJECT DATA
----------------------------------------------------------------------

Generated projects can include a compact machine-readable project code.

----------------------------------------------------------------------
28.3 DECODER KEY MODE
----------------------------------------------------------------------

User enters:

    alphabet,
    key,
    route,
    motif mapping,
    or password.

----------------------------------------------------------------------
28.4 ERROR REPORT
----------------------------------------------------------------------

Decoder should identify:

    probable framing error,
    invalid character block,
    parity failure,
    checksum mismatch,
    unexpected row width.

Better:

    "Possible error near character 14"

rather than:

    "INVALID."

----------------------------------------------------------------------
28.5 PHOTO DECODER - FUTURE
----------------------------------------------------------------------

Potential computer-vision pipeline:

    upload image
        ->
    perspective correction
        ->
    detect textile grid
        ->
    segment rows / stitches
        ->
    classify symbol states
        ->
    extract bits
        ->
    apply error correction
        ->
    decode

This will be difficult.

It is therefore obviously irresistible.

31. PATTERN-GENERATION ARCHITECTURE
======================================================================

Recommended software pipeline:

    PLAINTEXT
        ->
    NORMALIZER
        ->
    OPTIONAL CIPHER / ENCRYPTION
        ->
    SYMBOL ENCODER
        ->
    ERROR-PROTECTION LAYER
        ->
    LOGICAL GRID
        ->
    TEXTILE CARRIER
        ->
    PROJECT LAYOUT
        ->
    KNITTING TRANSLATOR
        ->
    OUTPUT

Why this matters:

    knit/purl
    colourwork
    cable
    lace
    bobble
    motif mutation

without rewriting the cryptography.

Likewise, the same carrier should accept:

    plaintext encoding
    Caesar ciphertext
    Bacon
    modern encrypted bytes

without rewriting the knitting engine.

Keep DATA and TEXTILE separate until late in the pipeline.

32. FLAT KNITTING, ROUND KNITTING, AND FABRIC ORIENTATION
======================================================================

This is a critical implementation detail.

A logical cell means:

    "the finished right-side surface should show state X"

It does NOT necessarily mean:

    "perform physical knitting action X."

When knitting flat:

    row 1 is worked in one direction,
    row 2 in the opposite direction.

The wrong-side operation may need to be inverted to create the intended
right-side texture.

Therefore:

    WRONG:
        bit 0 -> knit
        bit 1 -> purl

    BETTER:
        bit
        ->
        desired visible fabric state
        ->
        project construction
        ->
        physical stitch instruction

The translator needs to know:

    - knit flat or in the round,
    - right-side / wrong-side row,
    - chart reading direction,
    - desired visible face,
    - carrier technique,
    - whether chart cells represent actions or outcomes.

This distinction is one of the things that will make the app genuinely useful
rather than just a novelty text converter.

======================================================================
FINAL PROJECT STATEMENT - WORKING DRAFT
======================================================================

STITCH / SIGNAL explores the long and strange relationship between thread,
information, protest, memory, and secrecy.

It begins with historical textiles: red caps banned as resistance symbols,
clandestine prison embroidery, homespun boycotts, anti-colonial khadi,
suffrage banners, arpilleras, memorial quilts, protest hats, and the disputed
stories that grow around supposedly secret cloth.

It then asks what happens if we treat textiles deliberately as information
systems.

A purl can become a bit.
A cable can become a direction.
A knot can become a number.
A colour can become a symbol.
A motif can hide another motif.
A scarf can become ciphertext.

The project is therefore both archive and experiment:

    part history of resistance through craft,
    part introduction to codes and cryptography,
    part steganography playground,
    and part tool for turning messages into things you can actually knit.

The goal is not to prove that every old story about secret knitting is true.

The more interesting goal is to show what is documented, what is uncertain,
what people have imagined, and what thread is genuinely capable of carrying.

Because sometimes the craft contains the message.

Sometimes the craft IS the message.

And sometimes the scarf is encrypted.
